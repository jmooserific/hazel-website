# Voice and Tone

How Hazel talks in the App Store, on the website, in release notes, and in replies to people who write in. The principles govern the product; this governs the writing about it.

## The voice

Friendly, brief, and genuinely excited. One person who made something he loves, telling you about it the way he'd tell a friend: a sentence or two, a word explained if it needs it, a bit of real enthusiasm, then out of the way so you can try it.

The excitement is real, so let it show. "I hope you like it!" belongs here. Excitement just needs to point the right way. Saying how I feel about sharing the app is warm. Telling the reader how they'll feel ("you'll be amazed") is presumptuous. Making claims about the app ("the most realistic pieces anywhere") is bragging. Keep the first, drop the other two, and let the app do the persuading.

## Five rules

**Say less.** Write the short version first, and usually ship it. Detail is welcome when it helps the reader understand or enjoy something, not when it's there to show how much work went in.

**Explain, don't assume.** Hazel is for everyone, including people who have never thought about how a puzzle is cut. When a term might be unfamiliar, like stagger, explain it in a few plain words the first time it appears. The point is to bring people in on the fun. Don't explain words most people already know or can pick up from context, like knob, border, or edge piece. Explaining those talks down to the reader.

**Be glad, not boastful.** Express feelings in the first person and keep claims modest. "I had a lot of fun figuring this out!" is great. "The best puzzle app on the App Store" isn't. Describe what's there and trust the reader to like it.

**Speak as I.** There's one person behind Hazel, so don't write "we." Don't lean on the solo-developer story for sympathy either ("just one guy," "please support a small developer"). The app shouldn't ask anything of the reader.

**Never manipulate, including in the copy.** Principle 7 applies to the writing too: no urgency, no scarcity, no guilt-tinged rating prompts, no "don't miss."

## Words

Use warm, ordinary words.

Exclamation points are welcome, but use them sparingly, about one per piece of copy. Attach them to a feeling ("I hope you like it!"), never to a feature claim ("Realistic pieces!"). Don't use emoji in the App Store or release notes.

| Use | Avoid |
|---|---|
| cut, piece, knob, board, gallery | tiles, tokens, levels, challenges |
| make a puzzle, solve a puzzle | unlock, embark, dive in, experience |
| free | free forever, 100% free |
| I made, I fixed, I hope | we're excited to, we've been hard at work |
| what the thing does | stunning, immersive, ultimate, seamless, premium |

Two things stay out of user-facing copy. The first is any competitor's name. The second is internal vocabulary: nobody outside the repo knows what a Gemini piece or a false edge is. If a feature needs a public name, give it a plain one.

## By surface

**App Store subtitle.** Say what the app is.

**App Store description.** Start with what Hazel is. Follow with a short paragraph on what makes the pieces different, explained simply, then what isn't in the app. End warmly. Aim for about 120 words.

**Release notes.** Use one plain line per real change, and name the bug you fixed, including when you caused it. Never write just "bug fixes and improvements." Add a happy line when there's something you're happy about.

**In-app copy.** Prefer labels to sentences. Completion is the exception: a moment of warmth there is earned.

**Replies.** Start with thanks, then answer the question directly. If the answer is no, say so kindly and give the reason.

## What the app doesn't do, and price

State it once as a fact: no ads, no timers, no account, nothing to buy. Don't criticize other apps. Say it's free and move on without explaining why, since a justification invites people to look for the catch.

## Samples

**Subtitle:** Jigsaw puzzles from your photos

**Description:**

> Hazel turns your photos, or paintings from its small public-domain gallery, into jigsaw puzzles.
>
> Every puzzle is cut fresh, so no two pieces are alike. The knobs (the round tabs that lock pieces together) come in different shapes and sizes. Where cuts meet, they're slightly offset instead of forming a neat plus sign, the way a hand-guided saw would leave them. You can watch the cut happen when you make a puzzle.
>
> No ads, timers, or account. Nothing to buy. I hope you like it!

**Release note:**

> Pieces now keep their rotation while you drag them.
> Fixed the Gallery scrolling past its last row on iPad.

**Release note, with something to be happy about:**

> Pieces now click softly when they join. I spent way too long on that sound and I love how it turned out!

**Release note, owning a mistake:**

> Last update broke snapping on puzzles over 200 pieces. That's fixed now. Sorry about that.
